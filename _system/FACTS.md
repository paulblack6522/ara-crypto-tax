# FACTS.md — what this site is allowed to say about crypto tax

Every numbered statement below is verified against a primary source (irs.gov,
fincen.gov, or the Federal Register) and may be published. Each carries its
source. **If a sentence you want to write is not supported by a statement in
this file, cut it.**

Research date **2026-08-01**. Primary filing year in scope: **TY2025 (filed in
2026)**; TY2026 is in progress.

Two standing rules for how these are written on the page:

- Attribute the rule, never instruct the reader. Write "the IRS treats digital
  assets as property", not "you must report your crypto".
- Never present any of this as advice for a specific person's situation.

---

## 1. Foundational treatment

**F1.** The IRS treats digital assets as **property**, not as currency, and
general tax principles that apply to property transactions apply to them.
*Notice 2014-21 A-2; irs.gov "Digital assets"; Digital Asset FAQ 48.*

**F2.** A digital asset is any digital representation of value recorded on a
cryptographically secured distributed ledger or any similar technology. That
includes cryptocurrency, stablecoins and NFTs.
*Digital Asset FAQ 47; Instructions for Form 8949 (2025).*

**F3.** Holding period: one year or less is short-term, more than one year is
long-term, and the holding period **begins the day after** acquisition.
*Digital Asset FAQ 50; Virtual Currency FAQ 6 and 29.*

**F4.** An individual's net capital loss deduction is limited to **$3,000 per
year ($1,500 if married filing separately)**, and the excess carries forward.
*Topic no. 409.*

**F5.** Net capital gain from selling **collectibles** is taxed at a maximum
rate of **28%**. *Topic no. 409.*

**F6.** Taxpayers must keep records sufficient to establish the positions
taken on their returns, including receipts, sales, exchanges, dispositions,
transfers and fair market values.
*Digital Asset FAQ 95; Virtual Currency FAQ 46.*

## 2. The federal forms crypto lands on

**F7. Form 1040 — the digital asset question.** The 2025 Form 1040 asks, on
page 1 directly under Filing Status: *"At any time during 2025, did you: (a)
receive (as a reward, award, or payment for property or services); or (b)
sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of a digital asset (or a financial
interest in a digital asset)?"* Every individual filer must answer it Yes or
No, whether or not they hold digital assets.
*Form 1040 (2025); irs.gov "Reminders for taxpayers about digital assets".*

**F8. Form 8949 — Sales and Other Dispositions of Capital Assets.** Applies to
every sale, exchange or other disposition of a digital asset held as a capital
asset, including crypto-to-crypto swaps and paying for goods or services with
crypto. *Form 8949 (2025).*

**F9. Form 8949 gained six digital-asset-only checkboxes for 2025.**
Short-term Part I: box (C) short-term transactions *other than* digital asset
transactions not reported on Form 1099-B or 1099-DA; (G) reported on Form
1099-DA showing basis was reported to the IRS; (H) reported on Form 1099-DA
showing basis was **not** reported to the IRS; (I) digital asset transactions
not reported on Form 1099-DA or 1099-B. Long-term Part II carries the same
four as boxes (F), (J), (K) and (L). The form instructs that only one box may
be checked per page, so more than one applicable box means a separate Form
8949 page for each. *Form 8949 (2025).*

**F10. Schedule D (Form 1040)** summarises the Form 8949 totals. Short-term
totals carry to lines 1b, 2 and 3; long-term totals to lines 8b, 9 and 10.
*Form 8949 (2025).*

**F11. Schedule 1 (Form 1040)** carries ordinary, non-business digital asset
income. The 2025 Schedule 1 has a dedicated line — **Part I, line 8v,
"Digital assets received as ordinary income not reported elsewhere."**
*Schedule 1 (Form 1040) 2025; Digital Asset FAQ 110.*

**F12. Schedule C (Form 1040)** applies where digital asset activity is
carried on as a trade or business — mining as a trade or business, an
independent contractor paid in crypto, or a business that sells goods or
services for crypto. Net earnings are subject to self-employment tax.
*irs.gov "Digital assets"; Notice 2014-21 A-9; Digital Asset FAQ 60.*

**F13. Form 1040-X** amends a previously filed Form 1040, 1040-SR or 1040-NR —
for example an unreported disposal, a wrong answer to the digital asset
question, or a corrected Form 1099-DA. Generally, for a credit or refund it
must be filed within **3 years** (including extensions) after the original
return was filed, or within **2 years** after the tax was paid, whichever is
later. It can be filed electronically for those three forms.
*Instructions for Form 1040-X (12/2025).*

**F14. FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR)** applies to a US person with a financial
interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts whose
aggregate value exceeds **$10,000** at any time in the calendar year.
*Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR requirements.*

**F15. On crypto specifically, FinCEN Notice 2020-2 states that the FBAR
regulations do not currently define a foreign account holding virtual currency
as a reportable account**, so at this time such an account is not reportable
on the FBAR — unless it is reportable under 31 CFR 1010.350 because it holds
reportable assets besides virtual currency. The same notice says FinCEN
intends to propose amending the regulations to include virtual currency.
*FinCEN Notice 2020-2. Confirmed not superseded as of 2026-08-01.*

**F16. Form 8938** thresholds for specified foreign financial assets, living
in the US: unmarried or married filing separately — more than **$50,000** on
the last day of the year or **$75,000** at any time; married filing jointly —
more than **$100,000** or **$150,000**. Living abroad: **$200,000 / $300,000**
and **$400,000 / $600,000**.
*Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR requirements.*
⚠️ Publish the thresholds only. See DO-NOT-PUBLISH item **N2**.

**F17. Form 709** applies to a gift of digital assets above the annual
exclusion. The annual exclusion is **$19,000 per donee for 2025** and
**$19,000 per donee for 2026**.
*irs.gov "Digital assets"; Gift tax FAQs.*

## 3. Form 1099-DA — the biggest change for these filers

**F18.** The form is **Form 1099-DA, Digital Asset Proceeds From Broker
Transactions**. Brokers use it to report proceeds from — and in some cases
basis for — digital asset dispositions, to the taxpayer and to the IRS.
*irs.gov "Understanding your Form 1099-DA".*

**F19. Gross proceeds reporting is required for transactions effected on or
after January 1, 2025.** TY2025 is the first year the form exists.
*irs.gov "Digital assets"; IRS news release on the final broker regulations.*

**F20. Cost basis reporting begins for certain transactions effected on or
after January 1, 2026**, so basis first appears on forms furnished in early
2027. *irs.gov "Digital assets".*

**F21.** Brokers had to furnish the 2025 form to taxpayers by
**February 17, 2026**.
*irs.gov "Tax professionals can prepare now to assist their clients…".*

**F22.** The IRS states that **most 2025 statements will not provide basis**,
and that *"Basis must be calculated by taxpayers before their 2025 tax return
can be filed."* The Form 1099-DA page repeats it: *"You must calculate basis
before you file your tax return."*
*irs.gov "Tax professionals can prepare now…"; "Understanding your Form 1099-DA".*

**F23.** From 2026 forward: gross proceeds are mandatory for all digital
assets; basis is mandatory for digital assets that are **covered securities**
(generally acquired after 2025 in a custodial account) and voluntary for
**noncovered** ones. Where box 9 is checked, box 1g (cost or other basis) may
be blank. *Instructions for Form 1099-DA (2026).*

**F24.** The brokers who file it are custodial brokers, hosted wallet
providers, digital asset kiosks and certain payment processors.
**Decentralized and non-custodial brokers are excluded.**
*irs.gov "Digital assets".*

**F25.** The DeFi "front-end" broker rule was **disapproved by Congress** under
the Congressional Review Act — Public Law 119-5, signed April 10, 2025 — and
was removed from the Code of Federal Regulations effective July 11, 2025, with
no force or effect. *90 FR / Federal Register document 2025-12967.*

**F26. Broker de minimis thresholds are not taxpayer exclusions.** A broker may
omit designated sales of qualifying stablecoins where aggregate gross proceeds
do not exceed **$10,000** for the year, and specified NFT sales where aggregate
gross proceeds do not exceed **$600**; processor sales of digital assets use a
**$600** threshold. *Instructions for Form 1099-DA (2026).*

**F27.** The taxpayer still reports those transactions. The IRS answers
**yes** on stablecoin exchanges that do not appear on a Form 1099-DA (FAQ 100)
and **yes** on NFT dispositions under $600 that do not appear on one (FAQ 101).
*Digital Asset FAQ 100, 101.*

**F28.** The reporting duty does not depend on receiving a form: income, gain
or loss from all taxable transactions involving virtual currency must be
reported *"regardless of the amount or whether you receive a payee statement."*
*Virtual Currency FAQ 42; Digital Asset FAQ 108.*

**F29.** The IRS gave brokers penalty relief for good-faith 2025 reporting in
**Notice 2024-56**, extended a further year by **Notice 2025-33**. A practical
consequence for filers is that statements may arrive late, incomplete or with
no basis. *Notice 2024-56; Notice 2025-33.*

**F30. What a taxpayer should expect:** a Form 1099-DA or substitute statement
from each **US custodial** broker they transacted through — generally showing
**gross proceeds only** for 2025, with basis beginning to appear for 2026
transactions. **No Form 1099-DA arrives** from DeFi front-ends, non-custodial
wallets, generally from non-US exchanges, or for staking and mining rewards.
*Derived from F19, F20, F24, F25 and the Instructions for Form 1099-DA (2026).*

## 4. Wallet-by-wallet basis and Rev. Proc. 2024-28

**F31.** Under **§1.1012-1(j) of the 2024 final regulations (T.D. 10000)**,
basis and lot identification for all acquisitions and dispositions of digital
assets **on or after January 1, 2025** are determined **per wallet and per
account**, not across a universal pool.
*Notice 2026-20 §2, citing §1.1012-1(j)(6); Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §4.01(2).*

**F32. Rev. Proc. 2024-28** provides a safe harbor under §1012(c)(1) on which
taxpayers may rely to allocate unused basis of digital assets to the assets
held within each wallet or account **as of January 1, 2025** — the transition
from a universal or multi-wallet method to a wallet-by-wallet or
account-by-account method. It is effective **June 28, 2024**.
*Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §1 and §7; Notice 2026-20 §2.*

**F33.** It is available **only** to a taxpayer who holds remaining digital
asset units and has units of unused basis as of January 1, 2025, and may
**not** be applied to assets acquired by or transferred to the taxpayer on or
after that date. *Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §4.01(1)–(2).*

**F34.** It is applied **separately for each type of digital asset** — Bitcoin
is one type, Ether is another. Failing the requirements for one type does not
affect the safe harbor for another. *Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §4.01(4)–(5).*

**F35.** The allocation must be **reasonable**, made either as a **specific
unit allocation** (identifying particular units of unused basis and assigning
them to a wallet or account) or a **global allocation** (a pre-set ordering
rule applied mechanically). A method that lets the taxpayer exercise
discretion on or after January 1, 2025 is not a global allocation.
*Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §5.01, §5.02(2)(a)–(b).*

**F36. Deadline, specific unit allocation:** complete before the **earlier**
of the date and time of the first sale, disposition or transfer of that same
type of digital asset on or after January 1, 2025, **or** the due date
(including extensions) of the taxpayer's 2025 federal income tax return or
Form 1065. *Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §5.02(4).*

**F37. Deadline, global allocation:** the method must be described in the
taxpayer's books and records **before January 1, 2025**, with the allocations
completed before the **later** of those two dates.
*Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §5.02(5)(a)–(b).*

**F38.** The allocation is **irrevocable for all purposes of section 1012**.
*Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §4.02(6).*

**F39.** Records required: the total number of remaining units in each wallet
or account, the number of units of unused basis, the original cost basis of
each, and the acquisition date of the unit each unused basis was originally
attached to. *Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §4.02(4)–(5).*

**F40.** The safe harbor does not determine the **amount** of basis — that
still has to be substantiated under section 6001.
*Rev. Proc. 2024-28 §5.04.*

**F41. Default with no adequate identification: FIFO within that wallet or
account** — for broker-custodied units, the earliest units of that asset
acquired in that broker's custody; for non-custodied units, the earliest
purchase date of units in that wallet.
*Digital Asset FAQ 86, 87, 92; Notice 2026-20 §2.*

**F42.** Duplicating a private key does not merge wallets — duplicate key
records do not create a single wallet for federal tax purposes.
*Digital Asset FAQ 96.*

**F43. Current relief — Notice 2026-20** (Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-15,
April 6, 2026) extends the relief of Notice 2025-7 by a further year, so the
relief period runs **January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2026**. During it,
for units held **in the custody of a broker**, a taxpayer may make an adequate
identification under §1.1012-1(j)(3)(ii) **on the taxpayer's own books and
records** — either identifying the particular units no later than the date and
time of sale, or recording a standing order before the units are sold —
**without communicating it to the broker**. A taxpayer relying on the Rev.
Proc. 2024-28 safe harbor may rely on this relief only after that revenue
procedure's requirements are satisfied. The relief does **not** apply to units
not held in a broker's custody, does **not** apply to the §1.6045-1 broker
information-reporting rules, and may **not** be relied on for sales after
December 31, 2026.
*Notice 2026-20; IRB 2026-15.*

**F44.** A direct consequence of F43: for 2026 transactions, the acquisition
date and basis a **broker reports** may **not match** the lot identification
and basis on the **taxpayer's own books**. That is expected under the relief,
not an error. *Notice 2026-20 §2.*

⚠️ **Because of Notice 2026-20, Digital Asset FAQ 85, 88 and 89 — which say
that after December 31, 2025 the identification must be specified to the
broker — are superseded for calendar 2026. Do not publish them as current.**

## 5. Activity by activity

**F45. Staking rewards.** Rev. Rul. 2023-14 holds that where a cash-method
taxpayer stakes cryptocurrency native to a proof-of-stake blockchain and
receives additional units as validation rewards, the **fair market value of
the rewards is included in gross income in the year the taxpayer gains
dominion and control** over them, valued as of the date and time control is
gained. The ruling states the same applies where the taxpayer stakes through a
cryptocurrency exchange. Dominion and control is the ability to sell, exchange
or otherwise dispose of the units. Basis in the received units equals the
amount included in income. Reported as ordinary income on Schedule 1 line 8v,
or on Schedule C where it is a trade or business. **No Form 1099-DA is
required for staking**, so none will arrive.
*Rev. Rul. 2023-14; Notice 2024-57 §3.04; Instructions for Form 1099-DA (2026).*

**F46. Mining.** Notice 2014-21 A-8: when a taxpayer successfully mines
virtual currency, the fair market value as of the date of receipt is
includible in gross income. A-9: where mining constitutes a trade or business
and is not undertaken as an employee, the net earnings from self-employment
are subject to **self-employment tax** — Schedule C plus Schedule SE, with
business deductions. Where it is not a trade or business, the income is
ordinary income on Schedule 1. Basis in mined coins is the fair market value
included in income. *Notice 2014-21 A-8, A-9.*

**F47. Hard forks and airdrops.** Rev. Rul. 2019-24 holds that a taxpayer has
**no** gross income from a hard fork if no units of a new cryptocurrency are
received, and **has ordinary income** from an airdrop of a new cryptocurrency
following a hard fork if units are received. Income is the fair market value
of the new asset when received — generally when the transaction is recorded on
the distributed ledger, provided the taxpayer has dominion and control. Basis
equals the amount included in income. A **soft fork** produces no new asset
and therefore no income.
*Rev. Rul. 2019-24; Digital Asset FAQ 103–107.*

**F48. NFTs.** NFTs are digital assets, so buying, selling and swapping them
produces capital gain or loss reported on Form 8949 using the digital-asset
boxes, and an NFT disposition under $600 with no Form 1099-DA is still
reportable. **Notice 2023-27** is interim guidance: pending further guidance,
the IRS intends to determine whether an NFT is a section 408(m) collectible by
analysing whether the NFT's associated right or asset is a section 408(m)
collectible — a "look-through analysis". Its examples: an NFT certifying
ownership of a **gem** is a collectible; an NFT giving a right to use or
develop a plot of land in a virtual environment generally is not. Treasury and
the IRS are still considering the extent to which a digital file may be a
"work of art".
*Digital Asset FAQ 47, 101; Notice 2023-27.*
⚠️ Do not connect the look-through analysis to the 28% rate. See **N5**.

**F49. DeFi — what is settled and what is not.** **Notice 2024-57** identifies
six categories for which brokers are **not** required to file under section
6045(a) because they require further study: **wrapping and unwrapping,
liquidity provider transactions, staking transactions, transactions described
as lending of digital assets, transactions described as short sales, and
notional principal contract transactions.** The notice is explicit that this
is not a substantive answer — *"no inference is intended as to how an
identified transaction, or its component steps, is treated for substantive
Federal income tax purposes"* and inclusion *"is not intended to create an
inference that the identified transaction is or is not a sale of a digital
asset."*
*Notice 2024-57 §3.02–§3.07.*

**F50. What IS settled around DeFi:** an exchange of one digital asset for
another that **differs materially** is a taxable disposition; paying a
transaction or gas fee **in** digital assets is itself a disposition producing
gain or loss; and no Form 1099-DA will arrive from a DeFi front-end.
*Digital Asset FAQ 64, 66, 97; F25 above.*

**F51. Spending crypto on goods or services** is a disposition producing
capital gain or loss — *"capital gain or loss is recognized on the disposition
regardless of whether amounts qualify as transaction costs."* Gain or loss is
the difference between adjusted basis in the asset given up and the amount
realized (fair market value of what was received, reduced by allocable
transaction costs).
*Digital Asset FAQ 62–63; Virtual Currency FAQ 14–15.*

**F52. Transfers between a person's own wallets are not a taxable event** —
no income, gain or loss on moving digital assets between wallets, addresses or
accounts you own — **unless** digital assets are used or withheld to pay for
the transfer service, in which case that portion is a disposition. Digital
asset transaction costs do not include costs of transferring between your own
wallets. Basis and holding period follow the units across the transfer.
*Digital Asset FAQ 53, 81; Virtual Currency FAQ 38; irs.gov "Digital assets".*

**F53. Gifts received.** The recipient has no income on receipt; income is
deferred until they sell, exchange or dispose. Basis is **dual**: for computing
**gain**, the donor's basis plus any gift tax the donor paid; for computing
**loss**, the **lesser** of the donor's basis or fair market value at the time
of the gift. **If no documentation of the donor's basis exists, basis is
zero.** The recipient's holding period includes the donor's only if
documented.
*Digital Asset FAQ 75–77; Virtual Currency FAQ 31–33.*

**F54. Gifts given.** A gift above the annual exclusion ($19,000 per donee for
2025 and for 2026) requires Form 709.
*irs.gov "Digital assets"; Gift tax FAQs.*

**F55. Charitable donations.** No income, gain or loss is recognised on
donating digital assets to a qualified charitable organisation. The deduction
is fair market value at the time of donation where the asset was held **more
than one year**, and the **lesser of basis or fair market value** where it was
held **one year or less**. A **qualified appraisal is required for a claimed
deduction over $5,000**. CCA 202302012 concluded that where a taxpayer values
donated cryptocurrency using the value reported by an exchange rather than
obtaining a qualified appraisal, the reasonable cause exception will not
excuse noncompliance and the deduction will not be allowed. Deductions over
$500,000 require the appraisal attached to the return. The charity signs Form
8283 for donations over $5,000 and files Form 8282 if it disposes within three
years.
*Digital Asset FAQ 78–80; CCA 202302012; Virtual Currency FAQ 34–37.*

**F56. Wages.** The fair market value of virtual currency paid as **employee
wages**, measured in US dollars at the date of receipt, is subject to federal
income tax withholding, FICA tax and FUTA tax, and is reported on **Form W-2**.
*Digital Asset FAQ 61; Notice 2014-21 A-3, A-4.*

**F57. Contractor payments.** Digital assets received by an **independent
contractor** are self-employment income at fair market value in US dollars as
of the date of receipt, subject to self-employment tax — Schedule C plus
Schedule SE. Basis in the received units equals the amount included in income;
a later disposal is a separate capital transaction.
*Digital Asset FAQ 57–60; Virtual Currency FAQ 9–13.*

**F58. Section 1256 contracts.** Where digital assets **are** section 1256
contracts, section 1256 rules apply: gains and losses are treated as **40%
short-term and 60% long-term**, and brokers report them on Form 1099-B boxes
8–11. Section 1256 contracts include regulated futures contracts, foreign
currency contracts, nonequity options, dealer equity options and dealer
securities futures contracts, and are marked to market at year end.
*Digital Asset FAQ 51; Publication 550.*
⚠️ Do not publish a list of which crypto derivatives qualify. See **N4**.

**F59. Losses require a closed and completed transaction.** CCA 202302011
concluded that section 165 allows a deduction for losses evidenced by closed
and completed transactions, fixed by identifiable events, and actually
sustained during the taxable year; that a taxpayer who has not abandoned or
otherwise disposed of cryptocurrency which still has value has **not** sustained
a section 165 loss; and that even a sustained loss of that kind would be
disallowed because section 67(g) suspends miscellaneous itemized deductions
for taxable years **2018 through 2025**. **Abandonment requires both an
intention to abandon and an affirmative act of abandonment** — intention alone
is not enough, and non-use alone is not enough.
*CCA 202302011.*
⚠️ Do not extend the section 67(g) point past 2025. See **N3**.

**F60. Worthlessness, frozen accounts and theft.** The Taxpayer Advocate
Service states that where a digital asset investment becomes **completely**
worthless the loss is an **ordinary** loss, but the asset must be *completely*
worthless, not nearly worthless. For frozen accounts and platform
bankruptcies, no loss can be claimed until there is a closed and completed
transaction. For theft, the theft must meet the local jurisdiction's
definition of theft, and it applies in the year discovered. Capital losses
from actual sales remain limited to $3,000 ($1,500 MFS) net per year with
indefinite carryforward.
*Taxpayer Advocate Service — Digital Assets; Topic no. 409.*

## 6. The five most common and most costly mistakes

These are safe to publish as a section, phrased as neutral facts. All are
sourced above.

**M1. Answering the Form 1040 digital asset question incorrectly.** It sits on
page 1 and covers *receiving* digital assets as a reward, award or payment as
well as selling, exchanging or otherwise disposing of them. Every filer must
answer it, under penalties of perjury along with the rest of the return.
Buying and holding only, or transferring between one's own wallets without
paying fees in crypto, is a "No". *(F7, F52.)*

**M2. Treating "no Form 1099-DA" as "nothing to report."** Reporting is
required regardless of the amount and regardless of whether a payee statement
arrives. The $10,000 stablecoin, $600 NFT and $600 processor figures are
thresholds for **broker** reporting, not exclusions for the taxpayer. No form
arrives from DeFi front-ends, non-custodial wallets, most non-US venues, or
for staking and mining rewards. *(F26, F27, F28, F30.)*

**M3. Carrying a universal or multi-wallet basis pool into 2025 and later.**
The regulation applies per wallet and per account to everything on or after
January 1, 2025. Where pre-2025 unused basis was never attached to units in a
specific wallet, the safe harbor allocation generally had to be completed
before the first sale of that type of digital asset on or after January 1,
2025 — and it is irrevocable. Without it, the default is FIFO within each
wallet or account. *(F31, F36, F37, F38, F41.)*

**M4. Missing the taxable events that are not a sale for dollars.**
Crypto-to-crypto swaps of materially different assets, paying for goods or
services in crypto, and paying network or gas fees **in** digital assets are
each dispositions producing gain or loss. Separately, staking rewards, mining
and airdrops following a hard fork are ordinary income at fair market value
when dominion and control is obtained — in the year received, not the year
sold. *(F45, F46, F47, F50, F51.)*

**M5. Claiming a loss with no closed and completed transaction.** A decline in
value, a frozen account, a platform bankruptcy or a token that is "nearly
worthless" does not by itself produce a deductible loss. Abandonment requires
an affirmative act, not merely intent or non-use. *(F59, F60.)*

**Runners-up, all sourced above:** gifted assets defaulting to **zero basis**
when the donor's basis is undocumented (F53); a charitable deduction over
$5,000 denied outright for using an exchange price instead of a **qualified
appraisal** (F55); and, for 2026 transactions, a broker's reported lot and
basis **not matching** the taxpayer's books, which is expected under the
current relief rather than an error (F44).

## 7. ⛔ DO NOT PUBLISH

These are things crypto tax pages assert routinely that could **not** be
confirmed from an IRS or FinCEN primary source. Do not state them, do not
imply them, and do not answer them in the FAQ with a softened version.

**N1. "The wash sale rule does not apply to cryptocurrency."** What is
verified is only that §1091 and Publication 550 apply the rule to *"stock or
securities"* and that Publication 550 does not mention digital assets. There is
**no** IRS guidance putting digital assets outside §1091, and none putting them
inside it. The popular conclusion is a reading of the statute, not a published
IRS position, and it would not hold for a digital asset that **is** a security.
*If the site touches this at all*, the only permitted framing is: the statute
addresses stock or securities, and the IRS has not published guidance applying
it to digital assets generally — so we ask about it and handle it case by case.

**N2. "Crypto held on a foreign exchange is (or is not) reportable on Form
8938."** The current Instructions for Form 8938 (Rev. November 2021), the IRS
Basic Q&A on Form 8938, and the Form 8938/FBAR comparison page contain **no**
mention of digital assets, virtual currency or cryptocurrency. FBAR has an
explicit answer (F15); Form 8938 does not. Publish the thresholds (F16) and
the fact that we ask about it. Publish neither a yes nor a no.

**N3. "Section 67(g) still disallows worthlessness and abandonment losses
after 2025."** CCA 202302011's own words are *"for taxable years 2018 through
2025."* Reporting suggests a 2025 statute made the disallowance permanent for
tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, but the enacted statutory text
could not be retrieved and the IRS pages do not mention §67(g). State the
2018–2025 window only, exactly as CCA 202302011 states it.

**N4. Which specific crypto derivatives are section 1256 contracts.** FAQ 51
answers *"if my digital assets are section 1256 contracts"* — it does not say
which ones are. There is no IRS guidance identifying particular crypto
futures, perpetual swaps or options. Do not publish a list, and do not tell a
reader their perps are or are not 1256.

**N5. That NFT collectible treatment reaches the 28% capital gains rate.**
Notice 2023-27's look-through analysis is framed under **§408(m)**, the IRA
prohibited-investment rule. Topic 409 separately gives the 28% maximum for
collectibles. The linkage runs through §1(h)(5), but the notice itself does
not say its analysis governs the capital gains rate. Publish F5 and F48
separately; do not join them into a claim.

**N6. A release date for Notice 2026-20.** Cite **Internal Revenue Bulletin
2026-15, April 6, 2026**. Do not state a release date.

**N7. The Form 1099-DA furnishing deadline for TY2026 statements.**
February 17, 2026 is confirmed for TY2025 (F21). The equivalent 2027 date is
not confirmed. Do not state one.

**N8. "Congress has not passed a de minimis exclusion for small crypto
purchases."** No enacted exclusion was found, but that negative was verified
only by search. Phrase it as the IRS requirement instead — reporting is
required regardless of amount (F28) — never as a statement about what
Congress has or has not done.

**N9. Anything about state tax treatment**, including California. No state
source was consulted. The site is about **federal** reporting. Where state
residency appears in the intake form it is there to scope the engagement, not
because the site makes a claim about state rules.

**N10.** Anything about turnaround time, price, refunds, penalties avoided, or
audit outcomes. Not researched, not knowable, and covered by the outcome-claim
ban in `COPY-RULES.md` §2.4.

### Why these fields exist

The scoping drivers are **2.1 + 2.4 + 2.5 + 2.8** (venue and chain sprawl),
**4.1 + 4.2** (volume), **4.9 + 4.10** (basis gaps), **5.1 + 5.2** (the
wallet-by-wallet transition), and **1.1 + 5.5** (back years and amendments).
Those eleven fields alone sort most enquiries into a tier. Every other field
exists because a fact above (F31–F44, F45–F60) changes how the return is
prepared.
