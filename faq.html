Frequently asked questions

Sixteen questions about federal tax reporting for cryptocurrency activity, and how we work. Answers follow published IRS, FinCEN and Federal Register sources. Where a rule is unsettled, the answer says so.

How to read these answers

These answers describe rules the IRS has published. None of them is advice about a specific person's tax position, because your records are the thing that decides it. Unless a different year is named, the answers describe tax year 2025, filed in 2026. The questions that would decide your own position are the ones the intake form asks.

Whether there is anything to report

  • Buying a digital asset with dollars is not itself a disposition, and holding it is not one either. The Form 1040 question still has to be answered.

    The 2025 Form 1040 asks, on page 1 directly under Filing Status, whether at any time during 2025 you: received a digital asset as a reward, award or payment for property or services; or sold, exchanged or otherwise disposed of a digital asset or a financial interest in one. Every individual filer answers that question Yes or No, whether or not they hold digital assets, under penalties of perjury along with the rest of the return.

    Buying and holding only, or transferring between your own wallets without paying fees in crypto, is a No to that question. Paying a network fee in crypto during the year changes the answer, because disposing of the units used to pay the fee is a disposition.

  • Form 1099-DA, Digital Asset Proceeds From Broker Transactions, is the statement brokers use to report proceeds from, and in some cases basis for, digital asset dispositions, both to you and to the IRS. Gross proceeds reporting is required for transactions effected on or after January 1, 2025, so tax year 2025 is the first year the form exists. Brokers had to furnish the 2025 form to taxpayers by February 17, 2026.

    The brokers who file it are custodial brokers, hosted wallet providers, digital asset kiosks and certain payment processors. Decentralized and non-custodial brokers are excluded. So expect a form or a substitute statement from each US custodial broker you transacted through, and expect none from a DeFi front-end, a self-custody wallet, generally a non-US exchange, or for staking and mining rewards.

    Expect it to show proceeds and not basis. The IRS states that most 2025 statements will not provide basis, and its Form 1099-DA page puts it directly: you must calculate basis before you file your tax return. Cost basis reporting begins for certain transactions effected on or after January 1, 2026, so basis starts appearing on statements furnished in early 2027.

    Receiving no form does not mean there is nothing to report. Income, gain or loss from all taxable transactions involving virtual currency must be reported regardless of the amount or whether you receive a payee statement. The figures you may have read about, $10,000 for designated sales of qualifying stablecoins and $600 for specified NFT sales and for processor sales, are thresholds below which a broker may leave a transaction off the form. They are not exclusions for the taxpayer. The IRS answers yes on both stablecoin exchanges and NFT dispositions under $600 that do not appear on a Form 1099-DA still being reportable.

  • No. Moving digital assets between wallets, addresses or accounts that you own is not a taxable event, and no income, gain or loss is recognised on the move. Basis and holding period follow the units across the transfer, and the cost of transferring between your own wallets is not a digital asset transaction cost.

    There is one exception. Where digital assets are used or withheld to pay for the transfer service, that portion is a disposition of those units and produces gain or loss.

    Transfers still matter for records. Under the 2024 final regulations, basis and lot identification for acquisitions and dispositions of digital assets on or after January 1, 2025 are determined per wallet and per account rather than across a universal pool. A coin that moved has to be traceable to the wallet it now sits in. Duplicating a private key does not merge wallets either: duplicate key records do not create a single wallet for federal tax purposes.

  • This is a question your preparer will ask you rather than one a web page can answer, because it turns on what else is in the account and where the account is held.

    Here is what is published. FinCEN Form 114, the FBAR, applies to a US person with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign financial accounts whose aggregate value exceeds $10,000 at any time in the calendar year. On crypto specifically, FinCEN Notice 2020-2 states that the FBAR regulations do not currently define a foreign account holding virtual currency as a reportable account, so at this time such an account is not reportable on the FBAR, unless it is reportable under 31 CFR 1010.350 because it holds reportable assets besides virtual currency. FinCEN said in the same notice that it intends to propose amending the regulations to include virtual currency.

    Form 8938, for specified foreign financial assets, has its own thresholds. Living in the US: more than $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time, if unmarried or married filing separately; more than $100,000 or $150,000 if married filing jointly. Living abroad: $200,000 or $300,000, and $400,000 or $600,000.

    We do not publish a yes or a no on how digital assets sit against Form 8938, because the IRS has not published one. The intake form asks whether you hold a foreign account, and whether that account holds anything besides crypto, because that second answer is the one that moves the FBAR question.

How different activity is treated

  • Rev. Rul. 2023-14 holds that where a cash-method taxpayer stakes cryptocurrency native to a proof-of-stake blockchain and receives additional units as validation rewards, the fair market value of the rewards is included in gross income in the year the taxpayer gains dominion and control over them, valued as of the date and time control is gained. Dominion and control means the ability to sell, exchange or otherwise dispose of the units. The ruling states that the same applies where the taxpayer stakes through a cryptocurrency exchange.

    Basis in the units received equals the amount included in income, so a later sale is a separate capital transaction measured from that basis. The income is reported as ordinary income on Schedule 1, Part I, line 8v, or on Schedule C where the activity is carried on as a trade or business.

    Two practical points. The income falls in the year the rewards were received, not the year they were sold. And no Form 1099-DA is required for staking, so no statement will arrive to tell you the number; it comes out of your own records.

  • Notice 2014-21 answers this in two parts. When a taxpayer successfully mines virtual currency, the fair market value as of the date of receipt is includible in gross income. Where the mining constitutes a trade or business and is not undertaken as an employee, the net earnings from self-employment are subject to self-employment tax, which means Schedule C plus Schedule SE, with business deductions available against that income.

    Where mining is not carried on as a trade or business, the income is ordinary income on Schedule 1. Either way, basis in the mined coins is the fair market value that was included in income, so a later disposal is a separate capital transaction.

    Whether an operation is a trade or business is a facts question, and it is one of the things the intake form asks about.

  • An NFT is a digital asset, so buying, selling and swapping one produces capital gain or loss reported on Form 8949 using the digital asset boxes. An NFT disposition under $600 that does not appear on a Form 1099-DA is still reportable.

    One part of NFT treatment is not settled. Notice 2023-27 is interim guidance: pending further guidance, the IRS intends to determine whether an NFT is a section 408(m) collectible by analysing whether the NFT's associated right or asset is itself a section 408(m) collectible, which the notice calls a look-through analysis. Its own examples are an NFT certifying ownership of a gem, which is a collectible, and an NFT giving a right to use or develop a plot of land in a virtual environment, which generally is not. Treasury and the IRS say they are still considering the extent to which a digital file may be a work of art.

    We treat that as unsettled rather than picking whichever reading is convenient. Where a position has to be taken on an NFT, we tell you it is a position, tell you what we are doing and why, and put it in writing.

  • Some of it is settled and some of it is not, and knowing which is which is most of the value here.

    Settled: an exchange of one digital asset for another that differs materially is a taxable disposition. Paying a transaction or gas fee in digital assets is itself a disposition of those units, producing gain or loss. And no Form 1099-DA will arrive from a DeFi front-end, because the front-end broker rule was disapproved by Congress under the Congressional Review Act, Public Law 119-5, signed April 10, 2025, and was removed from the Code of Federal Regulations effective July 11, 2025, with no force or effect.

    Not settled: Notice 2024-57 identifies six categories of transaction that brokers are not required to file on under section 6045(a) because they require further study. They are wrapping and unwrapping, liquidity provider transactions, staking transactions, transactions described as lending of digital assets, transactions described as short sales, and notional principal contract transactions. The notice is explicit that this is not a substantive answer. It states that no inference is intended as to how an identified transaction, or its component steps, is treated for substantive Federal income tax purposes, and that inclusion is not intended to create an inference that the identified transaction is or is not a sale of a digital asset.

    So a page that tells you flatly how your liquidity pool position is taxed is telling you more than the IRS has. Where a position has to be taken on one of those six, we say that it is unsettled, explain what we are doing and why, and put it in writing.

  • A capital loss from an actual sale, swap or spend is reported on Form 8949 alongside the gains, and the two net against each other. An individual's net capital loss deduction is limited to $3,000 per year, or $1,500 if married filing separately, and the excess carries forward.

    A loss needs a closed and completed transaction. CCA 202302011 concluded that section 165 allows a deduction for losses evidenced by closed and completed transactions, fixed by identifiable events and actually sustained during the taxable year, and that a taxpayer who has not abandoned or otherwise disposed of cryptocurrency which still has value has not sustained a section 165 loss. Abandonment requires both an intention to abandon and an affirmative act of abandonment: intention alone is not enough, and non-use alone is not enough. The same memorandum adds that even a sustained loss of that kind would be disallowed because section 67(g) suspends miscellaneous itemized deductions for taxable years 2018 through 2025.

    On the harder cases, the Taxpayer Advocate Service states that where a digital asset investment becomes completely worthless the loss is an ordinary loss, but the asset must be completely worthless rather than nearly worthless. For a frozen account or a platform bankruptcy, no loss can be claimed until there is a closed and completed transaction. For theft, the theft must meet the local jurisdiction's definition of theft, and it applies in the year it was discovered.

    A price that fell is not by itself a loss, and this is one of the places where a claim made too early creates work later.

Records and past years

  • Taxpayers have to keep records sufficient to establish the positions taken on their returns, including receipts, sales, exchanges, dispositions, transfers and fair market values. When part of that is gone, the work is reconstruction from whatever does exist: exports from the venues still running, public wallet addresses, bank records of the dollars that went in and out, statements, and any spreadsheet you kept at the time.

    We ask what you actually have before we quote, and we tell you what is still missing before work starts rather than halfway through. If a gap cannot be closed, we tell you it is a gap and what that means for the position on the return, rather than filling it with a guess and saying nothing.

    Two things worth knowing in advance. Where a platform failed or froze withdrawals while holding your assets, no loss can be claimed until there is a closed and completed transaction, so the missing records and the loss are separate problems. And an undocumented history is not a neutral one: where a digital asset was received as a gift and there is no documentation of the donor's basis, basis is zero for computing gain.

  • Two ordinary pieces of preparation work cover past years, and both are things we do.

    A year that was never filed gets prepared and filed. A year that was filed but understated or omitted crypto activity gets amended on Form 1040-X, which amends a previously filed Form 1040, 1040-SR or 1040-NR: for example an unreported disposal, a wrong answer to the digital asset question, or a corrected Form 1099-DA. It can be filed electronically for those three forms. Generally, for a credit or refund, Form 1040-X must be filed within 3 years, including extensions, after the original return was filed, or within 2 years after the tax was paid, whichever is later.

    The underlying rule has not changed with time: income, gain or loss from all taxable transactions involving virtual currency must be reported regardless of the amount or whether you receive a payee statement.

    What we will not do is tell you in advance what the IRS will do about a past year, promise anything about penalties, or negotiate an amount owed. The intake form asks how the digital asset question was answered on your most recent filed return, whether any IRS notice has arrived, and whether you are currently under examination, because each of those changes how a catch-up is handled.

Working with us

  • We do not publish a price on this site, because a price written before anyone has looked at your records would be a number we made up.

    The sequence is: you send the intake form, we read it, we tell you what is missing, and we send a written quote. The quote is priced on the work involved, which in practice means how many venues and chains are in play, how many transactions and taxable disposals there are, how much of your cost basis is documented, and how many tax years are in scope.

    Nothing is charged until you accept the quote. Sending the intake form costs nothing and the quote costs nothing. If the scope turns out to be different from what the intake form described, we come back to you with a revised quote rather than adding to the invoice at the end.

  • We do not publish a turnaround time, and we would rather say that plainly than print a number we cannot stand behind.

    What the work takes depends on how many venues and chains are involved, how many transactions there are, whether cost basis is documented or has to be reconstructed, whether the year involves activity the rules do not settle, and how many years are in scope. A single US exchange with a statement is not the same job as six chains and a bridge history.

    The written quote states what is in scope. If something in the records turns out to be worse than the intake form suggested, we tell you when we find it rather than at the end.
