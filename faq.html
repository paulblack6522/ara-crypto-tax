Frequently asked questions

Sixteen questions about federal tax reporting for cryptocurrency activity, and how we work. Answers follow published IRS, FinCEN and Federal Register sources. Where a rule is unsettled, the answer says so.

These answers describe published IRS rules for tax year 2025, filed in 2026, and are not advice about a specific person's position.

Whether there is anything to report

  • Buying with dollars is not a disposition, and neither is holding. But the Form 1040 digital asset question still has to be answered Yes or No by every individual filer, under penalties of perjury.

    Buying and holding, or moving between your own wallets, is a No. Paying a network fee in crypto makes it a Yes, because the units spent on the fee are disposed of.

  • It is the statement brokers use to report your digital asset proceeds to you and to the IRS. Gross proceeds reporting starts with transactions on or after January 1, 2025, so 2025 is the first year it exists; brokers had to furnish it by February 17, 2026.

    Expect one from each US custodial broker, and none from a DeFi front-end, a self-custody wallet, a non-US exchange, or for staking and mining. Expect proceeds but not basis — the IRS puts it plainly: “You must calculate basis before you file your tax return.” Basis reporting begins for transactions on or after January 1, 2026.

    Receiving no form does not mean there is nothing to report.

  • No. Moving assets between wallets you own is not a taxable event, and basis and holding period follow the units across.

    One exception: where crypto is used to pay for the transfer, those units are disposed of. And transfers still matter for records — since January 1, 2025 basis is tracked per wallet and per account, not across one pool, so a coin that moved has to be traceable to where it now sits.

  • This one your preparer will ask you about, because it turns on what else is in the account.

    FinCEN Notice 2020-2 says the FBAR rules do not currently treat a foreign account holding only virtual currency as reportable — unless the account also holds other reportable assets, which is what moves the answer. FinCEN has said it intends to change this. Form 8938 has its own thresholds, and the IRS has published no answer on how digital assets sit against it, so neither do we.

How different activity is treated

  • Rev. Rul. 2023-14: the fair market value of validation rewards is income in the year you gain dominion and control — the ability to sell or dispose of them — valued at that moment. The same applies when you stake through an exchange.

    Your basis equals the amount included in income, so a later sale is a separate capital transaction. It is reported on Schedule 1 line 8v, or Schedule C if it is a trade or business. No Form 1099-DA is required for staking, so the number comes from your own records.

  • Notice 2014-21: the fair market value at the date of receipt is income. If the mining is a trade or business, it goes on Schedule C and is subject to self-employment tax, with deductions available; otherwise it is ordinary income on Schedule 1.

    Either way your basis is the value included in income, so a later disposal is a separate capital transaction.

  • An NFT is a digital asset, so buying, selling and swapping one produces capital gain or loss on Form 8949. A disposition under $600 that never appears on a Form 1099-DA is still reportable.

    One part is unsettled. Notice 2023-27 is interim guidance: the IRS looks through to whether the NFT’s associated asset is itself a collectible. Where a position has to be taken, we tell you it is a position, and put it in writing.

  • Settled: swapping one digital asset for a materially different one is a taxable disposition, and paying a gas fee in crypto disposes of those units. No Form 1099-DA will come from a DeFi front-end — that rule was repealed in 2025.

    Not settled: Notice 2024-57 lists six categories needing further study — wrapping and unwrapping, liquidity provider transactions, staking, lending, short sales and notional principal contracts — and is explicit that no inference should be drawn about how they are taxed. Anyone telling you flatly how your liquidity position is taxed is telling you more than the IRS has.

  • Losses from an actual sale, swap or spend go on Form 8949 and net against gains. An individual’s net capital loss deduction is capped at $3,000 a year ($1,500 if married filing separately), and the rest carries forward.

    A loss needs a closed and completed transaction. A price that fell is not a loss, and neither is a coin you still hold. For a frozen account or a platform bankruptcy, nothing can be claimed until the transaction closes. Claiming too early creates work later.
